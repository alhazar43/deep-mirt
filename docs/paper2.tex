\title{\vspace{-2em}When a Shared Representation Loses a Parameter:\\
Recovery of Low-Leverage Readouts in Amortized Encoders}
\author{Wenrui Yuan}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Amortized encoders increasingly replace direct estimation: a network reads a data
instance and emits both a latent state and interpretable parameters, trained on a
prediction loss. We show that this convenience can silently corrupt the parameters.
When two quantities are read off one learned representation and one has much weaker
leverage on the prediction than the other, the weak one is recovered poorly, not
because the data lack information about it (a per-instance oracle recovers it well), but
because gradient descent allocates the shared representation to the high-leverage
quantity during training. Giving the weak readout its own representation, or access to
the inferred state, restores its recovery. The effect is parameter-specific, vanishes
only at the joint infinite-data-and-training limit, and is a property of the
amortized-encoder arrangement rather than of any task: we reproduce it in a
non-psychometric encoder model and in neural item response theory (IRT), where the
leverage has a closed form, so we can predict which parameter is lost and see its
under-allocation in the representation geometry. A regularization control isolates the
effect from a separate overfitting decay. The consequence for measurement is direct: a
parameter read off a shared amortized representation can be an artifact of how the
representation was allocated, removable by decoupling.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Educational measurement increasingly runs through amortized encoders. Deep knowledge
tracing reads a learner's response history with a recurrent, memory, or attention
network and predicts the next response \citep{piech2015,zhang2017}; neural IRT attaches
interpretable readouts so the same forward pass exposes a learner's ability and an
item's difficulty and discrimination \citep{yeung2019,wu2020}. The appeal is
amortization: one model serves every learner and every item online, replacing a batch
calibration per item with a single pass, and returning psychometric parameters that
practitioners can act on.

That promise rests on a quiet assumption: that the readouts \emph{recover} the true
parameters, not merely that the model predicts well. The assumption is not automatic.
Prior work in this line observed, almost in passing, that an architectural separation
was necessary: ``without this separation, prediction quality holds but parameter
recovery does not'' \citep{yuan2025magpcm}. That observation is the seed of this paper.
We ask \emph{why} a shared representation costs a parameter its recovery, \emph{which}
parameter pays, and whether the phenomenon is specific to IRT or general to amortized
encoders.

Our answer is a representation-allocation effect, not a statistical one. When two
parameters are read off one learned representation and one has weak leverage on the
prediction, the weak parameter is under-recovered \emph{even where the data determine
it}. A per-instance estimator that shares no representation recovers it well, and the
amortized model itself recovers it at convergence; the shortfall is that, at any
practical training budget, gradient descent shapes the shared representation to serve
the high-leverage parameter and leaves the weak one a thin slice of capacity. We show
this in a minimal non-psychometric encoder model, where decoupling the weak readout
removes the deficit and a per-instance oracle proves the data were never the limit, and
in neural IRT, where the weak parameter is discrimination, its low leverage has a
closed form, and we can see it occupying about a tenth of the shared item code while
difficulty dominates.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{A recovery failure specific to amortized encoders.} A low-leverage readout
sharing the inferred representation is under-recovered, and the cause is representational
allocation, not the data's information: a per-instance maximum-likelihood oracle recovers
what the shared model misses.
\item \textbf{Generality.} The effect is a property of the amortized-encoder arrangement,
not of IRT. We reproduce it in a non-psychometric sequence model and in neural IRT, and
show a static (encoder-free) variant exhibits nothing.
\item \textbf{Characterization.} It is parameter-specific (difficulty, the
high-information control, is untouched), removable by two representational interventions
(a dedicated channel, or access to the inferred state), visible in the representation
geometry, and a finite-budget phenomenon; a regularization control separates it from a
distinct overfitting decay.
\item \textbf{A measurement caution.} A parameter read off a shared amortized
representation can be an allocation artifact; its magnitude in particular should not be
trusted unless the readout is decoupled.
\end{enumerate}

We are careful about what this is \emph{not}. It is not the classical fact that
low-information parameters have high estimation variance; that is about the data, whereas
our deficit persists where the data suffice and is fixed by changing the representation at
fixed data. It is not a claim that any reparameterization matters; smooth positive link
maps are interchangeable here. And it does not challenge estimation theory: the model is
identified and the estimator consistent. The new content is a property of the amortized
estimator's representation, which classical estimation does not possess.

\section{Background and Related Work}

\paragraph{Knowledge tracing and neural IRT.} Deep knowledge tracing reframed learner
modeling as sequence prediction \citep{piech2015}, and memory and attention encoders
refined it \citep{zhang2017,pandey2019}. Neural IRT attaches an IRT-style readout so the
encoded state yields ability and item parameters \citep{yeung2019}; amortized
variational IRT estimates them jointly \citep{wu2020}, and recent neural-IRT systems
separate student and item networks \citep{tsutsumi2024}. These works are our backbone and
our foil: they target prediction, and where they report recovery they report it for
ability, or hardcode discrimination, or share one representation across all parameters
without examining the coupling. We target the coupling itself.

\paragraph{Polytomous IRT and Fisher leverage.} The generalized partial credit model
\citep{vanderlinden1997,embretson2000} characterizes an item by a discrimination and
step thresholds, and supplies the closed-form leverage that makes our mechanism
predictable: the Fisher information for discrimination is suppressed by a squared lever
arm and vanishes where responses concentrate, so it is the structurally weak readout.
Scale indeterminacy in IRT also grounds our use of rank, not magnitude, as the recovery
metric \citep{embretson2000}.

\paragraph{Amortized inference and the amortization gap.} Amortized inference trades a
per-instance optimization for a single network at a known cost in quality, the
\emph{amortization gap} \citep{kingma2014,cremer2018}. Our finding is the
parameter-recovery, non-uniform version of that gap: it falls specifically on the
low-leverage parameter and is removable by decoupling. The closest psychometric
neighbors are amortized item factor analysis \citep{urban2021} and amortized joint
estimation \citep{molenaar2025}, which read all parameters off one shared representation
and recover loadings but never analyze the per-parameter coupling our oracle and
decoupling expose.

\paragraph{Representation learning and multi-task capacity allocation.} The effect is, at
bottom, an instance of multi-task interference on a shared trunk \citep{caruana1997,
ruder2017}: when readouts of unequal leverage share a representation, the dominant one
captures it. The multi-task literature offers \emph{algorithmic} remedies that rescale or
project gradients to stop a task dominating the trunk \citep{chen2018,yu2020}, and
predicts that incompatible co-learners hurt a shared representation
\citep{standley2020}. Our remedy is \emph{structural}: we remove the sharing rather than
balance the gradients, and we show the cost is geometric under-allocation rather than
gradient conflict (the pathway gradients are orthogonal). To our knowledge this lens has
not been applied to the recovery of interpretable measurement parameters.

\paragraph{Learning dynamics.} That recovery is leverage-ordered follows the dynamics
tradition: in deep linear networks modes are learned in singular-value order, high
leverage first \citep{saxe2014,advani2017}, with spectral and kernel analogs
\citep{rahaman2019,jacot2018}; the Hessian's few large eigenvalues correspond to the
high-leverage directions \citep{sagun2017}. The free-table invariant we use places the
effect in the transient, not the endpoint, and decoupling helps a \emph{learned} encoder
in the feature-learning regime \citep{damian2022}.

\paragraph{Identifiability and probing.} That the weak parameter is recoverable yet not
recovered is an identifiability-under-inductive-bias story: latent factors are not
recovered without an inductive bias \citep{locatello2019}, and a structural constraint
promotes separation \citep{higgins2017}; our decoupling is that bias, and our
per-instance oracle is a linear probe on the encoder output \citep{alain2017}.

\section{Methodology}
\paragraph{The amortized-encoder model.} An encoder $E_\phi$ reads an instance, a
sequence of observations, and produces a per-instance latent state. Item-level
parameters are read by linear heads off a learned item representation $e_j$ that also
feeds the encoder. Training minimizes a prediction loss only; no parameter is a
supervised target. Recovery is the sign-aligned rank correlation between recovered and
true parameters, since the latent scale is identified only up to an affine gauge. Two
representational arrangements are the levers we manipulate: \emph{shared vs.\ decoupled}
(the weak head reads the encoder's representation or its own) and \emph{static vs.\
dynamic} (the head is a function of the item alone, or may read the inferred state).

\paragraph{Closed-form leverage (neural IRT).} A 2PL/GPCM decoder makes leverage
explicit. With logit $z=\alpha(\theta-\beta)$, prediction $p=\sigma(z)$, and
cross-entropy loss, the residual is $r=p-y$ and
$\partial_\theta L = r\alpha$, $\partial_\beta L = -r\alpha$,
$\partial_\alpha L = r(\theta-\beta)$. The single-response Fisher informations are
$I(\theta)=I(\beta)=\alpha^2 w$ and $I(\alpha)=(\theta-\beta)^2 w$ with $w=p(1-p)$, so
discrimination $\alpha$ is the low-leverage parameter: its information is suppressed by
the squared lever arm and vanishes where responses concentrate ($\theta\approx\beta$).
IRT thus tells us, before any experiment, which head is weak.

\paragraph{A non-psychometric testbed.} To separate the arrangement from the task, we
build a minimal model with no IRT content: a recurrent encoder infers a per-instance
state from a sequence, and a high-leverage location and a low-leverage slope are read off
a shared item representation that also feeds the encoder. Leverage is a controllable
knob. A per-instance maximum-likelihood oracle with no shared representation gives the
information ceiling.

\paragraph{What the theory fixes, and does not.} Three points, with honest reach. (i)
Near an identifiable optimum each parameter recovers at a rate set by its information
(the linearization of gradient flow), so $\alpha$ is the slow mode; this is the starting
observation. (ii) \emph{The free-table invariant}: when item parameters form a free table
that reaches the optimal prediction, all gradient pulls are residual-linear and vanish
together, so the optimum is unbiased and the truth is recoverable, which we verify to
machine precision. Hence the finite-budget shortfall is not an information limit, the
linchpin of the paper. (iii) Any $C^1$ strictly-monotone positive link map induces a
time-reparameterization of one canonical trajectory, so the recovered ranks are
map-invariant; the link is not the lever. We do \emph{not} claim a conditioning-number
law: the Fisher conditioning number is collinear with the category count $K$, and varying
it over a $45\times$ range at fixed $K$ leaves the gap flat, so we attribute the growth
with $K$ to shared-channel capacity, not conditioning.

\paragraph{Synthetic protocol.} Data are generated from a known GPCM with $N=800$
respondents, $Q=60$ items, sequence length $T=60$, $\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and
$\alpha\sim\mathrm{LogNormal}(0,0.3)$. The encoder is an LSTM (results replicate on
Transformer and DKVMN); the decoupled arrangement gives discrimination its own wider
item code at matched total capacity. We report rank recovery with seeds and confidence
intervals.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{The effect is general, and not information-limited}
In the non-psychometric model, decoupling the low-leverage slope raises its recovery and
collapses its across-seed variance, while the high-leverage location gains nothing, so
the effect is parameter-specific (Table~\ref{tab:general}). The decisive control is the
per-instance oracle: at the leverage sweet spot it recovers the weak parameter at
$0.83$ while the shared model is stuck at $0.55$, so the shortfall is a
representation-sharing loss, not an information ceiling; at low leverage asymmetry both
arms reach the oracle and the gap closes. A static, encoder-free variant shows exactly
zero benefit, which locates the effect in the encoder's contention for the shared
representation rather than in shared readouts per se. The interpretation matters more
than the numbers: it is the \emph{arrangement}, not the data and not the task, that
loses the parameter.

\subsection{In neural IRT: allocation, not capacity}
Sweeping the width of a shared item code traces an ability--discrimination frontier
(Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}): ability falls from $0.97$ to $0.88$ as discrimination climbs
from $0.66$ to $0.91$, and no single width recovers both. A decoupled code that gives
discrimination its own representation sits above the frontier at matched total capacity,
even at several times the shared budget. The lesson is that the deficit is one of
allocation, not amount: adding capacity to the shared code does not buy both parameters,
but redistributing it does.

\caption{Allocation, not capacity. The shared family traces a frontier; the decoupled
model escapes it (upper right) at matched capacity.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{The under-allocation is geometric}
Probing the learned shared item code, discrimination occupies about $11\%$ of its
variance while difficulty dominates it (a linear probe recovers difficulty at $R^2=0.96$
versus $0.64$ for discrimination), and the item code carries no respondent-ability axis
(Table~\ref{tab:geom}). This is the allocation made literal: the representation spends
its capacity on the high-information parameters and leaves the weak one a thin slice.

\subsection{Reached then left, and the mechanism}
The both-high solution is reached early under sharing and then abandoned
(Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}): with a wide shared code, discrimination peaks at $0.92$ around
epoch~100 and decays to $0.85$ by epoch~500 ($10/10$ seeds decay, and the peak is a
genuine internal maximum per seed, not a coarse-grid artifact), while the decoupled code
rises and holds and beats sharing on $9/10$ seeds at the end. The mechanism is capture,
not conflict: the gradient on the shared item code from the ability pathway grows by an
order of magnitude over training (about $30\times$, 95\% CI $[20,43]$) while the
discrimination pathway stays flat, and the two pathways are near-orthogonal
(cosine~$\approx 0$). A regularization control closes the loop: adding LayerNorm to the
encoder strongly suppresses a \emph{separate} ability-overfitting decay (ability decay
shrinks from $-0.18$ to $-0.06$) but leaves the discrimination peak-then-decay essentially
untouched ($-0.075$ to $-0.074$). The discrimination effect is therefore the genuine
representation-allocation phenomenon, not a side effect of substrate overfitting.

\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{rigor_paper2_trajectory.png}
\caption{Discrimination recovery over training (10 seeds, 95\% CI). Wide-shared peaks
then decays; decoupled rises and holds. LayerNorm (right/overlay) removes the ability

\subsection{Two levers; difficulty is the control}
Both interventions that give discrimination more representational access relieve it.
Decoupling is above; state-conditioning, letting discrimination read the inferred state,
helps its rank recovery for $K\ge 4$, significant across twelve seeds (paired Wilcoxon
$p\le 0.007$, confidence intervals excluding zero) and growing with the number of
categories, with a clean null at $K=2$ (Table~\ref{tab:dynamic}). Difficulty, the
high-information control, is unmoved by either lever at every $K$, which rules out a
generic ``more flexibility helps'' reading and pins the effect to the low-leverage
parameter.

\subsection{Attenuation, not a confound}
Under sharing, recovered discrimination is attenuated and seed-unstable (rank
$0.81\pm0.058$ shared versus $0.86\pm0.013$ decoupled), but it is not contaminated: its
error correlates with neither ability nor difficulty in either arm (leakage within one
seed standard deviation of zero), consistent with the item code carrying no ability axis
to leak from. The failure mode is loss of resolution, not a hidden confound; decoupling
both raises the rank and stabilizes it.

\subsection{A finite-budget effect}
The shortfall vanishes only at the joint infinite-data-and-training limit. At a fixed
training budget in neural IRT, the decoupling advantage does not shrink as data grow
(Fig.~\ref{fig:nsweep}); in the encoder testbed it closes with data. We therefore report
it as a finite-budget phenomenon rather than a single rate-versus-data law.

a finite-budget signature.}

\subsection{Reading the experiments together}
One mechanism explains all of it. A low-leverage readout sharing the per-instance encoder
representation is starved of capacity (\S5.3) because the high-leverage pathway captures
the shared code (\S5.4); the data were never the limit (\S5.1, the oracle); the fix is to
give the readout its own representation or the inferred state (\S5.2); the failure is
attenuation, not a confound (\S5.6); and it is finite-budget (\S5.7). The same pattern
holds with no IRT semantics (\S5.1), so it is a property of the amortized-encoder
arrangement.

\section{Boundaries of the Claim}
We are deliberate about scope. All dynamics evidence is synthetic; recovering externally
calibrated item parameters from an error-rich real corpus is future work, and the
available real-data result is a stability proxy reported only as ordinal corroboration.
The contribution is a qualitative, general characterization, not a quantitative law: we
do not claim a conditioning-number rate law (it does not survive decorrelation from the
category count), and the data-versus-training dependence is model-dependent. The theory is
the differential rate together with the free-table invariant, not a closed-form rate
theorem. Several tempting stronger claims were tested and rejected: the exponential link
is not special; there is no population-limit dynamics law; the conditioning number is not
the lever; there is no ability-into-discrimination confound; and the effect is not a
generic shared-readout property but an amortized-encoder one.

\section{Discussion}
The result sits at a seam. It builds on learning-dynamics theory, where a mode's rate is
set by its information \citep{saxe2014}, and on the amortization gap \citep{cremer2018},
of which it is the parameter-specific version; it affirms classical IRT estimation,
adding a property of the amortized estimator's representation rather than correcting the
theory. It is not multi-task gradient conflict, since the pathway gradients are
orthogonal; the cost is geometric under-allocation, and where multi-task learning
rebalances gradients \citep{chen2018,yu2020} we remove the sharing structurally. The
closest precedent is the decoupling of a gain from a shared direction in weight
normalization \citep{salimans2016}; we supply the mechanism and tie it to a specific,
predictable parameter.

The consequence for measurement is concrete. A parameter read off a shared amortized
representation can be an artifact of how the representation was allocated: discrimination
from a shared neural IRT model is attenuated and unstable unless the readout is decoupled,
and its magnitude should not be trusted from a shared representation. The prescription is
to give low-leverage readouts their own representation, an architectural choice with a
predictable target, since IRT names the weak parameter in closed form.

\section{Conclusion}
In amortized encoders a low-leverage readout that shares the per-instance-inferred
representation is under-recovered, attenuated and geometrically under-allocated, and
decoupling or a dynamic channel restores it. The effect is general, parameter-specific,
not information-limited, and a finite-budget phenomenon, and neural IRT is the instance
where the weak parameter and its remedy are predictable in closed form. The practical
lesson is a validity caution: a parameter read off a shared amortized representation may
be an allocation artifact, removable by decoupling.
