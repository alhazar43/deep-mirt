\section{Setup}

\begin{frame}{Prediction is not the same as measurement}
We train a model to do one job, predict whether a learner answers the next
question correctly. Answers are right or wrong, or graded into a few levels.
\medskip

To predict well, the model works out three numbers on its own.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{score}, how strong a learner is.
\item \textbf{difficulty}, how hard a question is.
\item \textbf{sharpness}, how clearly a question tells apart strong and weak learners.
\end{itemize}
\medskip

We never supply these numbers and we never add a target for them. The model is
graded only on its answer predictions. The research question is whether good
prediction also gives back faithful measurement, and that is not guaranteed.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What we measure, and how}
On made-up data the three true numbers are known, so we can score the model's
recovered numbers against them.
\medskip

\begin{itemize}
\item We report \textbf{match in order} (rank agreement, 0 to 1), not raw values.
The scale and zero point of these numbers are not fixed by the data, only their
pattern is, so we compare order rather than raw values.
\item We track recovery \textbf{over training}, not just at the end, because the
path matters as much as the endpoint.
\item Every claim below carries a \textbf{control}, a capacity match, a negative
control, a matched starting point, or a matched null, stated on each slide.
\end{itemize}
\medskip

Four findings follow. Each is one observation, one hypothesis, one designed test,
one result.
\end{frame}

\section{Finding 1, sharpness is the hard number}

\begin{frame}{Finding 1, observation and hypothesis}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{s1_three_speeds.png}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.4\textwidth}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Observation.} Score and difficulty reach a near perfect match within the
first few to twenty training steps. Sharpness rises slowly and settles a notch
lower (about 0.93 against 0.97 and 0.99). It is consistently the last of the
three to come in.
\medskip

\textbf{Hypothesis.} Sharpness lags because the prediction barely depends on it.
A model can only pin down a number through that number's effect on the predicted
answer, so a number the prediction is insensitive to is learned slowly.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Finding 1, design and result}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{s4_why_hard.png}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Design.} Measure how much the predicted answer moves when each number is
nudged (its sensitivity, the Fisher information), and compare that ordering to
the recovery speeds. Repeat across answer levels.
\medskip

\textbf{Result.} The prediction leans heavily on score and difficulty and weakly
on sharpness. For a learner at a question's level the odds stay near a coin flip
whatever its sharpness, and far above or below the answer is near certain either
way, so only the band between is sensitive. The overall pull is small and shrinks
with answer levels, from about 1.0 at two levels to 0.19 at eleven.
\medskip

\textbf{Conclusion.} A number's leverage on the prediction governs how fast and
how well it is recovered. Sharpness is the low-leverage number.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Finding 2, a fix aimed at the hard number}

\begin{frame}{Finding 2, hypothesis and design}
\textbf{Hypothesis.} If sharpness is starved of signal, give it a richer readout.
Let each question's sharpness be a fixed part for the question plus a small extra
part that shifts with what the learner has answered so far.
\medskip

\textbf{Design.} Four model variants, holding everything else equal.
\begin{itemize}\footnotesize
\item fixed sharpness, narrow, the baseline.
\item fixed sharpness, wide, a \textbf{capacity-matched control} (same added size,
no history).
\item fixed plus history sharpness, the variant under test.
\item fixed plus history applied to \textbf{difficulty}, a \textbf{negative
control} on a high-leverage number.
\end{itemize}
We compare recovery across answer levels and over training, not at the endpoint
alone.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Finding 2, result}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{s2_sharpness_help.png}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Result.} The history part lifts sharpness recovery, and the lift grows
with answer levels, up to about $+0.20$ at eleven. The same change to difficulty
does almost nothing, and the capacity-matched wide baseline does not close the
gap where the history part helps (it pays in score recovery instead), so the gain
is specific, not just added size.
\medskip

\textbf{Two honest bounds.} The benefit is in speed and reliability, both
variants tie if trained to convergence, and the recovered quantity is the order
of sharpness, not its size. At two answer levels the extra part slightly hurts.
\medskip

\textbf{Conclusion.} A targeted readout helps the low-leverage number recover
faster and more reliably, more so as the task gets richer, without an endpoint
cost.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Finding 3, a tempting explanation, tested and ruled out}

\begin{frame}{Finding 3, hypothesis and design}
\textbf{Hypothesis.} Sharpness must stay positive, so it is read through an
always-positive formula. A natural guess is that one particular choice, the
exponential, is what makes sharpness learnable, because of how it rescales the
updates during training.
\medskip

\textbf{Design.} A fair contest between always-positive formulas (exponential,
softplus and its variants, sigmoid, and the cruder raw, relu, square).
\begin{itemize}\footnotesize
\item \textbf{Matched starting point}, every formula begins at the same effective
sharpness, so none is handed a head start.
\item \textbf{Per-formula learning-rate tuning}, so no formula simply had a better
rate.
\item A \textbf{direct test} of the training-step idea, optimizing sharpness
alone with the other two numbers fixed to truth, under each step rule.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Finding 3, result}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{s3_formula_distractor.png}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Result.} Under the fair contest, all the smooth formulas tie (within
about 0.02). Only the crude ones lag, the one that can still slip negative and
the one that flattens to zero and stops responding. The direct test of the
training-step idea also comes up empty, the step rules converge to the same
recovery.
\medskip

\textbf{Conclusion.} The exponential is not special. Staying positive matters and
being smooth matters, the particular formula does not. The hypothesis is ruled
out, a distractor removed. The direct test shows the positive formula does not
reshape the training in any decisive way. The benefit instead traces to the
dedicated readout of Finding 2.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Finding 4, how far to trust the history part}

\begin{frame}{Finding 4, design, result, and limit}
\textbf{Design.} Plant a real, known link between a question's sharpness and the
learner's strength, then ask the history part to recover it. Compare against a
matched control with no planted link, so any baseline structure is subtracted.
\medskip

\textbf{Result.}
\begin{itemize}\footnotesize
\item It recovers the \textbf{direction} of the planted link.
\item It recovers the \textbf{size} only weakly, about 4 percent of the true effect, a roughly 25-fold shrinkage.
\item It shows a little structure even when nothing is planted, so a small
adjustment can be noise.
\end{itemize}
\medskip

\textbf{Conclusion.} Read the history part for direction, not for amount. This
matches Finding 1, the prediction has too little leverage on sharpness to pin its
magnitude, only its ordering.
\end{frame}

\section{Why, the mechanism}

\begin{frame}{Why the low-leverage number is the slow one}
The four findings share one cause, set out in the supporting analysis.
\medskip

\begin{itemize}
\item The prediction's sensitivity to sharpness works out to the learner-question
gap times the answer's uncertainty, (score minus difficulty) times $p(1-p)$. It is
zero when the learner sits at the question's level, and zero far away, so its
overall pull is small and shrinks as answer levels grow.
\item That weak signal sets the \textbf{rate} at which sharpness is learned, not a
permanent error. With unlimited data and training the gap closes, which is why the
fix is a speed gain, not an endpoint gain.
\item The same weak signal is why magnitude is not recovered, only order.
\end{itemize}
\medskip

This is a modest, local account that explains the pattern. It is the support, not
the headline. The headline is the measured behavior.
\end{frame}

\section{Takeaway}

\begin{frame}{Takeaway}
\begin{block}{One line}
A model trained only to predict answers learns each question number only as well
as the prediction leans on it.
\end{block}
\medskip
\begin{itemize}\small
\item Score and difficulty are easy. Sharpness is the low-leverage number, learned
slowly and only in order.
\item A fixed plus history readout helps sharpness recover faster and more
reliably, more so with more answer levels, with no endpoint cost. It gives the
order of sharpness, not its size.
\item We tested and ruled out the idea that a special positive formula was the
key. Staying positive and smooth matters, the formula does not.
\end{itemize}
\medskip
\footnotesize \textbf{Scope.} Made-up data, three to ten repeats, one model family.
\textbf{Next.} A check on real learner data, where the true numbers are unknown
and we test stability instead of recovery.
\end{frame}
